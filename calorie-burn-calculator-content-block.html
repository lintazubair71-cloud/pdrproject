How calories burned are calculated (the formula)

We use the standard MET (Metabolic Equivalent of Task) method recommended by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM):

Calories burned = MET × 3.5 × body weight (kg) ÷ 200 × minutes

  • MET = how intense the activity is vs. resting (1 MET ≈ sitting still).
  • A quick estimate: calories per hour ≈ MET × your weight in kg.
  • For net calories (burn above what you'd use at rest), replace MET with (MET − 1).

MET values come from the 2011 Compendium of Physical Activities. Results are estimates (±10–15%); real burn also depends on fitness, age, body composition, intensity and terrain.

Frequently asked questions

How many calories did I burn?

Enter your body weight, pick your activity, and set how long you exercised — the calculator above estimates calories burned using the MET formula. As a rule of thumb, a 155 lb person burns roughly 350 calories per hour walking briskly, 490 jogging, and 686 running at 6 mph.

How do I calculate calories burned during exercise?

Multiply the activity's MET value by 3.5 and your weight in kilograms, divide by 200, then multiply by the minutes you exercised: MET × 3.5 × kg ÷ 200 × minutes. Or use the fast estimate: calories per hour ≈ MET × your weight in kg.

What is a MET?

A MET (Metabolic Equivalent of Task) measures exercise intensity. 1 MET is your energy use sitting at rest; an 8-MET activity burns about 8× that. Higher MET = more calories burned per minute.

How long does it take to burn 100 calories?

About 17 minutes of brisk walking, 12 minutes of jogging, or 9 minutes of running for a 155 lb person. Heavier people reach it faster.

What is the afterburn effect (EPOC)?

EPOC (Excess Post-exercise Oxygen Consumption), or the "afterburn effect," is the extra calories your body burns recovering after intense exercise like HIIT or heavy strength training. It can add roughly 6–15% on top of the calories burned during the session.

Is this calorie burn calculator accurate?

MET-based estimates are typically within ±10–15% for most people. Actual calories burned vary with fitness level, age, body composition, exercise technique, and environment. Use the result as a reliable estimate, not an exact figure.
